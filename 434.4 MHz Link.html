434.4 MHz Link

Type a message on a computer, or let the transmitter send a hard-coded one, and it appears on an LCD at the other end of a radio link you build from discrete parts. Two ATmega32 chips handle the data. Everything in the radio path is a custom circuit: a Colpitts oscillator on the transmitter and a super-regenerative detector on the receiver.

At 434 MHz, stray capacitance and wire length move the carrier by several percent, so the trimmers CT and CT1 are essential. Measure the real carrier with an SDR dongle before you trust it.

ATmega32 transmitter

ATmega32

The UART collects characters into a 32-byte buffer until it sees a newline. If nothing has arrived, it sends the hard-coded message HELLO FROM CAIRO every 2 seconds. It wraps the text in a packet, Manchester-codes it, and sends it three times with a 30 ms gap so a missed packet does not lose the message. A button on PD2 forces the hard-coded message.

Colpitts oscillator

discrete

A common-base Colpitts. L1 and the series pair C1 + (C2 + CT) set the frequency. L1 is an air coil of 2.5 turns of 1 mm wire on a 4 mm form (about 14.5 nH by Wheeler). Cd is 100 pF at the supply. CT trims the carrier onto 434.400 MHz.

T6

RF buffer

discrete

A BFR92A buffer. A small 2.2 pF coupling capacitor loads the oscillator very lightly, so the antenna and filter cannot pull its frequency when you touch the wire. The 330 Ω emitter resistor is not bypassed, which keeps the stage from clipping and sets the output level. The collector feeds the T7 filter directly, with no coupling capacitor in between.

T7

Low-pass filter

discrete

Without a filter the second harmonic at 869 MHz is only about 9 dB below the carrier, far above any spurious limit. Five reactive parts bring it down about 40 dB. The filter is designed so the T6 collector sees about 100 Ω, instead of a 50 Ω load behind a coupling capacitor, so it takes the full output of T6. The 100 pF capacitor at the end blocks DC. There is no resistive pad: it wasted most of the power and was the main load on T6.

Serial input

The transmitter takes its message from a serial port. Open any terminal program on a computer (PuTTY, the Arduino serial monitor, screen), set it to 9600 baud, 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit, type up to 32 characters and press Enter. The text appears on the receiver's LCD a moment later. If nothing is connected, the transmitter keeps sending its hard-coded message, so the link works with no computer at all.

The ATmega32 runs its UART from the 8 MHz crystal. UBRR = 8,000,000 / (16 × 9600) − 1 = 51, which gives 9615 baud, 0.16 % fast. That is well inside what the line tolerates.

Packet format

A packet is 7 bytes of framing plus the message. It takes 92 ms for 16 characters and 156 ms for 32, at 2 kbit/s. The preamble gives the data slicer time to settle. The sync word marks the start, the length tells the receiver how many characters follow, and the CRC-8 rejects the noise a super-regenerative receiver makes between packets.

AA AApreamble2D D4syncADDR1 byteLEN1 to 32MESSAGELEN ASCII bytesCRC-8poly 0x07

The CRC-8 covers ADDR, LEN and the message. Manchester coding sends a 1 as high then low, and a 0 as low then high, with 250 µs per half-bit. The signal always has equal on and off time, so the receiver's 10 ms average sits at the middle and the threshold follows it.

The free-running LC oscillator drifts with temperature and battery voltage, by perhaps a few hundred kHz. That still keeps it inside the band, but a drifting carrier leaves the receiver detuned, so keep the transmitter away from heat and re-trim the receiver after the first hour of running.

Bring-up order

  1. Build T1 and R9 first. Check 4.95 V and 3.25 V with no RF parts fitted.
  2. Build T5 alone. Put a short loop of wire near an SDR dongle and turn CT until the carrier shows 434.400 MHz. Pull the coil turns slightly apart to raise the frequency, squeeze them to lower it.
  3. Add T6, T7 and T8 one at a time. Check the second harmonic at 868.8 MHz is at least 40 dB below the carrier. Without T7 it is only about 9 dB below.
  4. Build R4 and look at its output with a scope or a frequency meter. It should read about 219 kHz.
  5. Build R3, R2 and R1. With the transmitter off, you should hear or see noise at the R5 output. Turn RV1 until the noise level peaks.
  6. Start with the two boards 0.5 m apart. The LCD should show the hard-coded message. Trim CT1 for the strongest signal.
  7. Connect the serial port, type a short message, press Enter, and watch it replace the hard-coded text. Then step out to 5 m.
